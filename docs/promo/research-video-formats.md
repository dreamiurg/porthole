## A. Build-story structures (2–10 min), with example timings

**General retention framework (secondhand, aggregated SEO/creator-economy blogs — not
creator-specific, treat as consensus advice rather than fact):**
- Three-phase open: 0–5s pattern interrupt, 5–15s "payoff promise" (concrete value stated),
  15–30s commitment hook (information gap / proof / demo start).
  [YouTube First 30 Seconds: The 3-Phase Opening Structure](https://prepublish.ai/guides/first-30-seconds)
- Alternative hook formula: 3–8s tension/provocative statement, 10–15s why-it-matters, 5–10s
  payoff tease. Benchmark cited: "Aim for 70%+ retention at the 30-second mark."
  [YouTube Hooks That Actually Hold Viewers Past 30 Seconds](https://voclify.io/blog/youtube-video-hook-writing-hold-viewers-past-30-seconds)
- Named failure patterns to avoid: generic greetings, logo bumpers, meta-commentary, slow
  context builds, apologies, engagement asks before value.
  [YouTube Hook Analyzer](https://www.overseeros.com/blog/youtube-hook-analyzer)
- Pattern interrupts: recommended roughly every 5–10s early, tapering later; a bigger interrupt
  suggested around the 25–35s mark, "right where research found the first major drop-off zone."
  Caveat noted in the same corpus: "Audiences over 35 actually retain worse" with constant
  zoom/whoosh cuts — match interrupt density to audience.
  [Editing for Retention: Pacing, Pattern Interrupts, B-roll](https://monitoryt.com/blog/editing-for-retention),
  [Pattern interrupt — Grokipedia](https://grokipedia.com/page/Pattern_interrupt_video_editing)
- CTA placement advice: best placed "60–70% into the video, not at the end."
  [The Hook Formula You Need to Skyrocket Retention](https://www.jamiewhiffen.co.uk/start-getting-views/the-hook-formula-you-need-to-skyrocket-retention)
- Chapter guidance (generic, not from any named maker channel): "10–25 chapters for most long
  videos... 4–6 minute average duration per chapter" — this is aimed at 20+ minute videos, not
  directly applicable to a 2–10 min promo, include only as background.
  [YouTube Chapters for Long Videos](https://instantviews.net/youtube-chapters-long-videos)

**Named creators — what I could actually source (mostly secondhand synthesis, not direct
transcripts):**

  "Problem-Agitation-Solution," pairing an emotional hook with real engineering. A Stanford Daily

## B. Real footage + 3D/CAD explainer hybrids

- **Branch Education** (secondhand, from a "Born to Engineer" profile and the channel's own
  site, which counts as primary for its own description): built by a former semiconductor
  engineer; technique described as rebuilding "microchips, printed circuit boards, and physical
  models in photorealistic 3D" from real teardowns and high-resolution images, so viewers "step
  inside working hardware." Content is structured as a "branched" tree — a top-level device video
  branches into sub-explainers of its components. Every explanation is stated to be sourced from
  "technical manuals, source papers, and patents."
  [Branch Education — official site](https://branch.education/),
  [Branch Education — Born To Engineer](https://www.borntoengineer.com/top-engineering-videos-branch-education)
- No sourced material was found specifically on **Real Engineering**, **Lesics**, or
  Wendover-style transition mechanics (i.e., how they cut between live footage and render). I
  could not confirm claims about "the render becoming the real object" as a technique used by any
  specific named channel — treat that as a generic editing concept, not a sourced fact about these
  channels.
- **Match cuts / render-to-real transitions** (generic film-editing reference, not maker-video
  specific): a match cut carries the viewer between shots via a shared visual, motion, or
  compositional element — e.g. matching framing, subject shape, or continuous motion across the
  cut. Cited technique: "continue the momentum of the previous subject's movement" into the next
  shot. This is the standard mechanism for a render "becoming" its physical counterpart (cut on
  matching silhouette/position, not just a hard cut).
  [What Is a Match Cut? — Boris FX](https://borisfx.com/blog/what-is-a-match-cut/),
  [Match Cuts & Creative Transitions — StudioBinder](https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/match-cuts-creative-transitions-examples/)
- **iFixit-style teardown** (secondhand, general description, not a specific technique
  breakdown): iFixit publishes teardowns as both article and video, and the exploded view is
  described in marketing-technique sources as "the standard way to show what's inside a premium
  product." No sourced detail found on iFixit's specific shot pacing or explainer segment length.
  [Category: Video — iFixit](https://www.ifixit.com/News/category/video)
- I found no sourced numbers on how long real-vs-rendered segments typically run in hybrid maker
  videos (e.g., "renders run 20–40s before cutting back to camera"). Do not assert a specific
  duration in the video script without treating it as an estimate.

## C. Product-film shot vocabulary (open hardware / small gadgets)

- **Crowd Supply's own campaign-video guide** — this is a primary, first-party source (Crowd
  Supply telling its own hardware-campaign creators how to shoot), the strongest single source
  in this research pass:
  - Length: "Videos should be at most one to two minutes long," ideally "short and on task —
    ideally under a minute."
  - Core info must land "within first 10–15 seconds."
  - Shoot 16:9, minimum 1280×720 (1080p preferred), always on a tripod/mount, use off-camera
    lights, lock exposure/focus to avoid distracting refocus hunts, avoid digital zoom (move the
    camera/object instead).
  - "Keep your shots simple and uncluttered" — one clear subject per shot; only move the camera
    "slowly, smoothly, and deliberately," and only when needed.
  - Suggested 5-part structure: intro (identity + value prop) → top features (live demo solving
    a problem) → use cases → specs/details → CTA (sign up for updates).
  - Combine plain-background product shots with "lifestyle/demo shots showing off the product
    in use."
  - Minimize text overlays; prefer voice-over. Use "royalty-free or properly licensed music."
  [Campaign Photography: Creating Stills and Videos — Crowd Supply](https://www.crowdsupply.com/guide/project-video)
- **Flipper Zero, Playdate, Teenage Engineering, Tidbyt, M5Stack, Pwnagotchi, Arduboy** — I was
  not able to source specific shot-by-shot breakdowns of their launch/product videos. What I did
  find (secondhand, press coverage, not shot analysis):
  - Playdate: Panic's 2019 reveal was "an online blog post and teaser video that showcased early
    prototypes in action" (TechCrunch/Axios-style coverage, paraphrased); the device itself is a
    2.7" B&W screen with a crank, later reported at "over 80,000 units sold."
    [Playdate (console) — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Playdate_(console))
  - Teenage Engineering: design/brand aesthetic (not video technique specifically) is described
    as monospaced type throughout, a five-color palette, "brutalist honesty about what a thing
    is," and promotional material in monochrome recalling "German New Wave... and Berghain" — a
    Medium essay's characterization, not TE's own statement. Useful only as an aesthetic
    reference, not shot vocabulary.
    [The Product Design of Teenage Engineering — Medium](https://medium.com/@ihorkostiuk.design/the-product-design-of-teenage-engineering-why-it-works-71071f359a97)
  - Flipper Zero, Tidbyt, M5Stack, Pwnagotchi, Arduboy: no usable sourced material on their
    product-video shot vocabulary specifically; searches returned product pages, GitHub repos,
    and unrelated teardown coverage. Do not cite specifics about these products' videos.
- **Generic shot vocabulary terms** (industry glossary / stock-photography sources, not tied to
  any named product video, but useful as a checklist of what this genre typically reaches for):
  exploded view (separates components in space in one frame, used to "show the thought put into
  a design" or "heighten visual impact for product launch videos"), turntable shot (motorized
  turntable for continuous product rotation, common in "product demonstrations... marketing
  videos"), macro/close-up (needs strong even lighting and a tripod at high magnification).
  [30 3D Exploded View Video Techniques](https://advids.co/blog/30-creative-3d-exploded-view-video-examples-to-showcase-technical-insights),

## D. Narration choices for a solo dad/hobbyist, and kids on camera

- **On-camera vs. voice-over** (secondhand, creator-economy blog synthesis, generic — not maker-
  specific): voice-over/faceless format removes on-camera performance overhead (no studio setup,
  no on-screen "acting"), which the source frames as an advantage for solo/small-team creators.
  Estimated production time for a traditional (non-AI) voiceover on a ~10-minute video: "one to
  three hours" scripting, "two to four hours" recording with retakes, "one to two more hours"
  post — stated as generic industry figures, not tied to any specific creator, treat as rough
  order-of-magnitude only.
  [AI Voice for YouTube Complete Creator Guide](https://narrationbox.com/blog/ai-voice-for-youtube-creator-guide)
  A separate piece frames it simply: being both on-camera talent and director "is not for
  everyone," and voice-over lets a creator be "acoustically active as an invisible narrator
  without appearing."
  [Vlog vs Voice-Over Video](https://voiceovermaker.io/blog/difference-between-vlog-and-voice-over-video)
- **Kids appearing safely** (secondhand, parenting/creator-safety guides, consistent with
  YouTube's own written policy — the policy citation is closer to primary since it's YouTube's
  own rules):
  - Common creator-safety recommendation: don't show kids' faces or use real names; a workaround
    cited elsewhere is having kids "narrate videos where only hands and reviewed items are
    visible."
  - If names are used at all, first name only, and no school, city, friends, teachers, or family
    details.
    [Safe, Secure, & Smart — Fairplay for Kids](https://fairplayforkids.org/pf/safe-secure-smart-videos/)
  - YouTube's own child-safety policy (primary — this is YouTube's platform rule, not advice)
    prohibits minors' content filmed in private spaces like bedrooms/bathrooms, and restricts
    content showing minors "soliciting contact from strangers," doing dares/challenges, or
    "revealing personal details about minors."
    [Child safety policy — YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2801999?hl=en)
  - For this project: hands-only shots of kids' hands on the device/soldering, no names, no
    faces, aligns with both the general creator-safety guidance and YouTube's own policy language
    above. I found no sourced examples of specific well-known maker videos that use this exact
    "hands only" technique for their own kids — the recommendation is generic child-safety advice,
    not a pattern observed in a named maker video.
- **Tone for maker audiences** — no single sourced article addressed "humble, specific, shows
  failures" as an explicit named formula. The closest sourced material is indirect: Wintergatan's
  documentation of setbacks as a deliberate, ongoing part of the format (section A), and the
  general retention-editing corpus's warning against "apologies or disclaimers" as an opening
  (i.e., show the failure, don't apologize for it, in the body of the video — but don't open on
  an apology). Treat "humble and specific, show real failures" as this researcher's synthesis of
  those two data points, not a directly sourced claim.

## E. Repurposing: Shorts / Reddit clips / vertical reframing

(All secondhand — SEO/creator-tooling blog consensus, not platform-official or from a named
maker channel. No specific per-platform length numbers were returned by search beyond the
general aspect-ratio spec below; do not invent specific second-counts for Shorts/Reels length
beyond what's here.)
- Selection: pick self-contained moments that "open on a claim" and survive being cropped to
  vertical; YouTube Analytics retention spikes/rewatch points are suggested as a way to find
  which moments are already proven.
- Reframing: target vertical canvas 1080×1920 (9:16); "intelligent reframing should keep faces
  in view," and creators should "preview the crop and... safe zones" against platform UI overlays.
- Captions: "most viewers scroll with sound off," so captions plus a front-loaded hook are
  treated as close to mandatory; captions should be "accurate, readable, and timed to speech."
- Suggested general workflow: analyze source → select self-contained moments → extract →
  reframe vertical → caption → check safe zone → stagger releases.
  [How to Turn Long Form Videos into YouTube Shorts at Scale — Opus](https://www.opus.pro/blog/youtube-shorts-at-scale),
  [YouTube Shorts From Long Video: How to Cut Them — Descript](https://www.descript.com/blog/article/how-to-create-youtube-shorts-from-existing-video)
- Applied to this project: the 3D/CAD explainer segments (exploded view, fly-through, screen
  close-up) are natural standalone vertical clips since they're already self-contained visual
  beats without dialogue dependency — this is my inference from the above, not a sourced claim
  about 3D-render repurposing specifically.
- No usable material was found on Reddit-clip conventions specifically (Reddit itself unreachable,
  and searches for Reddit-specific video-clip norms returned only generic cross-platform advice
  that doesn't distinguish Reddit from Shorts/Reels). Treat Reddit-cut guidance as the same as the
  generic vertical-clip advice above, with no confirmed Reddit-specific norms found.

## F. Audience reaction to AI-assisted builds / disclosure

- **Direct maker-community source (Hackaday, closer to primary — I fetched the article and
  quoted it directly):**
  - On capability and attitude: "a competent maker or hacker can leverage these new AI tools to
    help bring more of their projects across the finish line — and that's something we've got a
    hard time being against."
  - On growth in submissions: "We've also seen a[n] uptick in submitted projects that utilized
    AI in some way," citing examples like an LLM-assisted port of Macintosh System 7 to x86.
  - On risk: "an over-reliance on AI tools can be dangerous. In the best case, the user is
    deprived of the opportunity to actually learn."
  - Separately, Merriam-Webster's 2025 Word of the Year was "slop," defined as "computer-
    generated garbage content" — cited by Hackaday as a real community concern, distinct from
    legitimate AI-assisted builds.
  [2025: As The Hardware World Turns — Hackaday](https://hackaday.com/2026/01/05/2025-as-the-hardware-world-turns/)
  - Note (secondhand, from the search summary, not independently verified by fetch): "many
    readers want to see what folks are doing with LLMs and LLM-assists," while Hackaday tries to
    separate cool hacks from poor-quality "AI slop" projects.
- **Cautionary example of disclosure done badly (not a maker/hardware channel, but the closest
  sourced example of audience reaction to AI use in a build-and-explain YouTube video; secondhand
  press coverage of a real, verifiable event)**: science/education YouTuber Hank Green admitted
  using ChatGPT for script research on a July 2026 video; backlash followed when a segment
  "sounded as though it had been generated by an AI chatbot." Coverage states his own explanation
  was that ChatGPT was used "primarily... to locate academic papers," not to write the video's
  arguments, but that some viewers "were disappointed because they expect educational creators to
  independently verify information rather than rely on AI-generated research." He described
  himself as "mortified" by the reaction and said he would pause his personal channel.
  [Hank Green apologizes for ChatGPT overuse — Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/08/04/did-hank-green-use-ai-chatgpt-youtube-social-media/),
  [A Complete Breakdown Of The Hank Green ChatGPT AI Scandal](https://www.thegamer.com/hank-green-chatgpt-scandal/)
  Lesson applicable here (my synthesis, not a direct claim from any one source): the backlash was
  about the appearance of hidden/undisclosed AI use surfacing after the fact, and about it
  touching the audience's trust in factual claims — not about AI use itself. This project's use
  of Claude Code for firmware/software is a different category of claim (build tooling, not
  factual research), which is a meaningful distinction to make explicit on camera.
- **General platform framing (secondhand, aggregated SEO-policy blogs, consistent with each
  other but generic)**: "Platforms are not penalizing AI use — they are penalizing hidden AI
  use," and disclosure is framed as trust-building over time rather than something with a
  built-in punishment. YouTube's actual policy requirement (primary, platform rule) covers
  *realistic synthetic/altered media* specifically (e.g., a real person's face or voice being
  faked) — it does not, as far as sourced, require disclosure merely for using an AI coding
  assistant to help write firmware.
  [How we're helping creators disclose altered or synthetic content — YouTube Blog](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/disclosing-ai-generated-content/)
- I found **no sourced example of a hardware/maker YouTube video specifically disclosing use of
  an AI coding assistant** (Claude Code, Copilot, ChatGPT, etc.) and describing audience reaction
  to that disclosure. This is a real gap — searches for "Claude Code hardware project YouTube

  gadget build. Do not present a hardware-specific AI-disclosure case study as if one exists;

## G. Ten concrete takeaways for this project

1. **Open on the device, not on you.** The strongest sourced advice across every framework
   (Crowd Supply, generic retention guides) is: state the concrete thing in the first 10–15
   seconds. For this project that's the screen-first shot of the puck running Pets Club/Biscuit,
   not a "hi, I'm a dad who..." preamble — that kind of opener is explicitly named as a failure
   pattern in the retention research (section A).
2. **State the "why" early and specifically**, then delay the full reveal. Both the generic hook
   formula and Mark Rober's reported three-step formula agree on: name the goal/challenge fast,
   then hold the payoff (the finished case + both games) for later. Section A.
3. **Show the actual failures**, not just the success — Wintergatan's format (setbacks
   documented as part of the build, not edited out) is the best-sourced example of "journey"
   storytelling here, and it lines up with your real story (radar build → wanting a bigger
   screen → discovering the board world → picking Waveshare). Section A.
4. **Use match cuts to bridge real footage and Blender renders**, cutting on matching
   silhouette/position/motion rather than a hard cut, so a render can plausibly "become" the
   real case in one motion. This is a generic film technique, not confirmed as any specific
   channel's method, but it's the standard mechanism for exactly this kind of transition.
   Section B.
5. **Keep individual render segments purposeful and short.** No sourced duration exists for
   ideal render-segment length in hybrid maker videos (a real gap, section B) — treat this as an
   open creative decision, not something to justify with invented research.
6. **Borrow the Crowd Supply product-film checklist for the maker-community promo film**: tripod-
   locked shots, exposure/focus locked before rolling, one clear subject per shot, deliberate
   slow camera moves only, combine plain-background hero shots of the case with in-use/lifestyle
   shots (kids' hands on the screen). This is the single most concrete, first-party source found
   in this pass. Section C.
7. **For kids: hands-only, first names optional but no surnames/faces/location details.** This
   matches both generic creator-safety advice and YouTube's own child-safety policy language,
   and it's compatible with the emotional core of the story (a dad building with his kids)
   without exposing them. Section D.
8. **Voice-over over on-camera talking-head is the lower-effort, equally credible choice** for a
   solo creator — sourced generically, not as a maker-specific finding, but consistent with the
   project's real assets (you already have narration-worthy footage: soldering, device, kids'
   hands) rather than needing a studio-lit face-cam setup.  Section D.
9. **Cut the long video into Shorts/Reddit clips using the already-self-contained beats**: the
   3D exploded view, the camera fly-through, and the screen close-up demo are naturally vertical-
   croppable and don't depend on dialogue continuity — reframe to 1080×1920, caption everything
   (most viewers watch muted), and pick clips from whatever segment gets the strongest reaction
   in the full cut. Section E.
10. **Disclose the AI-assisted build plainly and specifically, framed as a tool used for a
    build, not as a claim about facts.** The one concrete cautionary case found (Hank Green,
    section F) shows backlash follows *hidden* AI use touching *factual* claims, not disclosed
    use of a coding tool; Hackaday's own community commentary is explicitly open to AI-assisted
    hacking as long as it's shown, not hidden. A simple on-screen line — "used Claude Code to
    write the firmware, built everything else by hand with my kids" — fits the "AI use is fine
    when it isn't hidden" pattern found in both sources. No sourced example exists of this exact
    disclosure in a hardware-maker video, so treat this recommendation as reasoned extrapolation,
    not a proven pattern.
